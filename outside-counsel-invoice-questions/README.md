# Outside-counsel invoice questions

When a law firm’s invoice arrives, legal operations has one worksheet ready: every line set against the agreed rates, the approved matter budget and the company’s own billing guidelines, each difference written as a neutral question for the firm. Nothing is sent, paid or rejected until people decide.

*Workflow template · Legal and contracts · version 0.1.0 · [Workflows Directory](https://fullypowered.ai/workflows/outside-counsel-invoice-questions/)*

## The outcome

Every outside-counsel invoice gets the same careful read, line by line, without anyone spending an afternoon on it. The worksheet sets each line beside the company’s own records and lists what does not match: a rate different from the agreed rate for that date, two entries that look like the same work, several tasks billed under one time, a timekeeper who is not on the matter’s staffing list, more people billing for one meeting than the number the company checks against, a phase that has gone past its budget, a line dated outside the invoice’s period. Each item names its invoice line and the record it was compared with, so anyone can check it in a minute.

Each item comes with a plain question for the firm, ready for the matter’s lawyer to confirm. The questions ask; they never accuse. A question such as “please give the time for each task” is meant to be easy for the firm to answer and to keep the working relationship good.

This is preparation, and it stays preparation. The workflow template gives no legal advice. It decides nothing about legal meaning, merit, validity, privilege, conflicts or deadlines, and it never says what an engagement letter or a guideline means. It gives no due date or time limit for paying or questioning an invoice, and counts no date. It sends, files, signs and calendars nothing, and it approves, rejects, reduces and pays nothing. It never says whether anything in the worksheet supports or rules out paying, holding, deducting, withholding or disputing an amount, and it never vouches for the draft: asked for a verdict, an amount, a date, a case or what to do, it names the person who decides and points to their line of the worksheet, and adds nothing. It writes no letter, dispute notice or demand to the firm, even for a lawyer to edit: the questions are the only words it prepares for the firm. Every item is a question, never a finding that the firm has broken its guidelines. Every worksheet is headed as a draft for the named lawyer, with a line for each question that lawyer must confirm.

## Who starts the work, and when

The legal operations manager starts it when an outside-counsel invoice for a matter arrives, before anyone approves it for payment. The named lawyer, the in-house counsel responsible for the matter, confirms, changes or strikes each question. The legal budget owner, often the general counsel, decides payment once the firm has answered, and finance pays what was approved.

Where the company has no lawyer of its own, the named lawyer is outside counsel from a different firm than the one whose invoice is questioned. That lawyer confirms the check map and every question, exactly as in-house counsel would, before anything is sent. The firm being questioned never reviews its own questions, and the worksheet never tells the business whether the invoice is right. Without such a lawyer, the workflow template prepares nothing and asks for one.

## What they supply

- **The invoice:** its header and its lines as an export, with the column headings the template names. If a heading does not match, the workflow template stops and asks.
- **The agreed rate table** for the engagement, with who approved it and when. Rates come from this table only.
- **The approved staffing list** for the matter.
- **The approved matter budget,** by phase, with what was billed before this invoice.
- **The company’s billing guidelines,** as numbered rules, and a short check map, approved once by a lawyer acting for the company and never by the firm whose bills it checks, that says which rule feeds which check.
- **Earlier invoice lines** for the same matter, if you want repeats from last month caught too.

The workflow template carries no rates, no benchmarks and no billing rules of its own. Everything it compares comes from your records.

## What comes out

One worksheet, marked as a draft for the named lawyer:

- **Records used**, each with its approver and date, and any check that could not run.
- **Tie-out:** the lines add up to the invoice total, hours times rate equals each line’s amount, and every line is dated inside the invoice’s period. If the lines do not add up, nothing else is prepared.
- **Five sections:** rate differences, possible duplicate entries, block entries, staffing items, and the budget by phase with any overrun. Each reads “None.” when empty.
- **Questions for the firm:** one per item, in fixed patterns that quote the line and the record, cite a guideline rule by its number only, and ask the firm to explain or confirm.
- **Lawyer to confirm:** a line for each question, a line for the check map, a line that the records used are current, a line that any time limit for the invoice is the lawyer’s to check, and a line where the lawyer releases or holds back the section for the legal budget owner. Until the named lawyer signs, nothing in the worksheet is sent or acted on.
- **For the legal budget owner, once the lawyer has signed:** the lines with no question (not approved, only not questioned), the lines with one, the guideline rules not checked, a plain note that the worksheet lists questions only and does not say whether to pay, hold or reduce anything, and that payment is theirs to decide after the firm answers, with any due date left to the named lawyer. It reaches the budget owner only when the named lawyer releases it, which matters most where the company has no lawyer of its own and the budget owner is not a lawyer.

The legal operations manager reads the worksheet against the invoice and gives it to the named lawyer. Only the questions the lawyer confirms are sent, by a person, from their own email.

## What has to change for your company

The check map is the heart of this. Your named lawyer (in-house, or, where you have no lawyer of your own, outside counsel from another firm; never the firm whose bills it checks) reads your billing guidelines once and decides which rule feeds each check: rates, the staffing list, time by task, how many people may bill for one meeting, and raising a budget overrun in advance. Rules that need judgement stay off the map and are listed on every worksheet as not checked, for a person to read. The same lawyer reads the fixed question wording once against your guidelines, so no question asks about an approval step your rules do not have.

Your rate table needs an owner and dated rows, so a rate change on the first of the month is read correctly. Ask your firms to put a phase code on every line, so the budget comparison works. Map your e-billing export’s columns to the template’s headings once. Name who confirms when the matter’s lawyer is away, and who may see the worksheet, since invoice descriptions tell the story of the legal work.
