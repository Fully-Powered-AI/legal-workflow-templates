# Contract intake brief

When another party's agreement arrives, counsel gets a one-page intake brief: what it is, read from its titles, the parties as named, what is missing, the requester's purpose and date, and the reviewer your own routing rules name. It comments on no term; counsel decides.

*Workflow template · Legal and contracts · version 0.1.0 · [Workflows Directory](https://fullypowered.ai/workflows/contract-intake-brief/)*

## The outcome

Every incoming agreement reaches counsel in the same shape, on one page, before anyone has read a clause. Counsel sees what arrived and what did not, who is named and where the names disagree, what the business needs it for and by when, and whose desk the company's own rules put it on. The chasing that usually eats the first day of a review, for the missing exhibit, the unsigned signature page or the right company name, is already a short list of questions for the requester.

The brief reads what a document is from the title on its first page and the title of every exhibit and schedule, never from the words in its clauses and never from what the requester calls it. A services agreement stamped confidential on every page and sent over as "their NDA" is listed as what its first page says it is, and routed that way.

This is preparation for a lawyer, and it stays preparation. It gives no legal advice. It decides nothing about legal meaning, merit, validity, privilege, conflicts or deadlines, and nothing about whether the agreement is acceptable or worth signing. It does not comment on a single term or offer any wording. It sends, signs, files and calendars nothing, and nothing goes to the other party. Every brief is headed as a draft for the named lawyer, with a line for each point that lawyer confirms.

## Who starts the work, and when

The contracts manager, the legal operations lead, or the operations person who routes contracts starts it when a supplier, customer or partner sends its own agreement for the company to sign. The intake owner confirms the routing. The named lawyer decides everything else: in-house counsel, or, where the company has no lawyer of its own, the outside counsel it uses. In that case the brief is addressed to outside counsel and goes to them; it is never handed back to the business as an answer. If the company has no lawyer at all, the brief is marked not ready for counsel and waits for one; it is not a review of the agreement and says nothing about whether to sign.

It is not for a law firm's new-client inquiries, which [inquiry handoff](https://fullypowered.ai/workflows/inquiry-handoff/) prepares, or for deciding whether to answer a buyer's request for proposal, which is [bid or no-bid and RFP response plan](https://fullypowered.ai/workflows/bid-or-no-bid-and-rfp-response-plan/). Letters, claims, notices and court papers go straight to counsel as received. A question such as "is this fine to sign?" is recorded in the brief for counsel and not answered, and the reply to it points to counsel without picking out any of the brief's findings as bearing on the answer.

## What they supply

- **The agreement as received:** every page and every attachment the other party sent, as text or a PDF with readable text, with the file name it came under.
- **The request:** the requester's email, ticket or intake form, saying what the agreement is for, when it is needed and why, which of your companies is meant to sign, the expected value, and any earlier agreements with the other party.
- **Your routing rules:** written, dated and approved by counsel, saying which title words and which facts send an agreement to which reviewer, and what happens when no rule or two rules apply.
- **The named lawyer** who receives the brief.
- **Your list of your own legal entities,** if you keep one.

## What comes out

One brief, marked as a draft for counsel, in seven parts:

- **What arrived:** the main title, each attachment's title and page count, every attachment the clauses mention that did not arrive, anything mentioned that lives elsewhere, and the markings on the document's face.
- **Parties, as named:** each name copied from the opening, the signature blocks and the attachments, with every difference set out.
- **Signature pages:** each signature block as found, and any party without one.
- **The request,** in the requester's own words.
- **Proposed routing:** each of your rules shown as matching, not matching, waiting on a missing attachment, or not yet applicable for want of a fact, and the reviewer the rules name.
- **Ask the requester for:** factual questions only, each tied to the line it fills.
- **Counsel to confirm:** one line for each point counsel settles, including the review scope and the requester's date.

A check block closes it: pages read against the document's own footers, the tally of attachments, the routing rules used with their dates, and a statement that no term was read for its meaning. The intake owner confirms the routing and sends the questions personally. The brief goes to counsel, not to the requester.

## What has to change for your company

Your routing rules are the heart of it, and counsel writes them. They say which words in a title send an agreement where, which facts such as value or team change that, who decides when no rule applies or two point different ways, and when the rules are next reviewed. A short set that sends everything unusual to counsel is a good start. The workflow template brings no routing of its own.

You also name the lawyer who receives each brief and who covers when that lawyer is away, keep your list of your own legal entities current, and match the request fields to your intake form. Decide where briefs are kept and how the intake owner records the routing confirmation.

We sit with the person who routes your contracts and the lawyer who reviews them, write down how intake really works, and set it up in the AI product your company has approved: ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot or Gemini. Confirm first that your company's policy allows agreements from other parties to be used in it.
